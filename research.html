    I Am So Tired! I Don't Know What to Do! Survey Fatigue and Financial Literacy: Results from a Randomized Experiment

    With Kim P. Huynh, Marcel Voia. Draft, June 2026

    Abstract

    We use a randomization of question placement to estimate the causal effect on financial literacy results. We find that when financial literacy questions are placed at the end of a survey, respondents are more likely to answer “Don’t know.” The increase in “Don’t know” responses comes largely at the expense of correct responses. We find that this leads to a drop in financial literacy by 5%-15%. This research suggests a measure of financial literacy that is adapted to account for survey length.

    2024 Methods-of-Payment Survey Report: Cash in an Era of Alternatives

    With Marie-Hélène Felt, Angelika Welte. June 2025

    Abstract

    The Methods-of-Payment (MOP) Survey tracks consumer use of cash and other methods of payment. We present core findings from the 2024 MOP Survey, highlighting results from both the survey questionnaire and subsequent three-day shopping diary. Although cash holdings have increased in nominal terms, we find that cash usage remains unchanged since 2020. Mobile and other alternative payment methods continue to grow in importance. The 2024 MOP Survey also collects new data on how consumers perceive bank note quality.

    Migration and the Classroom: The Effects of Immigration on the Test Scores and School Experience of Native Students in Europe (Undergraduate Thesis)

    Solo-authored. May 2024

    Abstract

    With six waves of the OECD’s Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) and an IV strategy, I use recent variations in the proportion of immigrant students across European countries to ask the following questions: How does attending school with more immigrants affect native student test scores, and how does this change self-reported attitudes towards school and immigration? As native students attend schools with more than 1% PISA-sampled immigrants (9% immigrant students on average), I find that their Math, Science, and Reading test scores decrease by around 4-6%. Academic effects are heterogeneous, with evidence that students in countries less open to migration face a more substantial test score effect. When redefining immigrant presence as the presence of immigrant peers who have plausibly different educational backgrounds than native-borns, the test score effect increases in magnitude. These last two results suggest that country-level educational infrastructure and classroom student heterogeneity could be important channels. When it comes to social outcomes, the effect on self-reported school experience is more ambiguous. However, evidence suggests that native students exposed to more immigrant peers may report more positive attitudes towards different cultures, with students in immigrant-heavy schools self-assessing positive attitudes up to 10% more frequently. Put together, the effect of immigrant peers on native students’ academics is small and negative, but not overwhelmingly so, while exposure to immigrants may even increase native prosociality.
